Normalisation

Normalisation is the process of structuring a relational table so each fact is stored once, in one place, with no redundant copies that can drift out of sync. Transactional systems normalise aggressively; analytics systems often deliberately denormalise. Understanding both directions is what makes you a useful modeller.

First normal form (1NF)

A table is in first normal form when every column holds a single atomic value and every row is unique. Repeating groups are forbidden: you cannot have host_name_1, host_name_2 columns, and you cannot store a comma-separated list of amenities in one cell and call it done.

Violations of 1NF are rarer in modern warehouses than they were in spreadsheet exports, but they still appear when someone pivots in Excel and loads the result as a table.

Second normal form (2NF)

2NF applies to tables with a composite primary key. Every non-key column must depend on the whole key, not just part of it. If your grain is (order_id, product_id) and you store product_name in that table, you violate 2NF because product_name depends only on product_id.

Third normal form (3NF)

3NF removes transitive dependencies: no non-key column may depend on another non-key column. If listing_id determines host_id, and host_id determines host_name, then storing host_name on the listings row duplicates data that belongs on a hosts table.

Airbnb example: decomposing a wide listings table

Imagine you loaded listings and hosts into one wide table for convenience. Every listing row repeats its host's name and superhost flag. That is a classic 3NF violation: host_name depends on host_id, not on listing_id.

Before — one denormalised table (violates 3NF):

-- listing_id | listing_name | room_type | host_id | host_name  | is_superhost
-- 3176       | Cozy loft    | Entire    | 501     | Maria      | true
-- 4821       | Sunny studio | Private   | 501     | Maria      | true   <-- host_name duplicated
-- 9012       | Beach house  | Entire    | 772     | James      | false

Step 1 — split to 1NF/2NF/3NF by entity:

-- listings (grain: one row per listing)
-- listing_id | listing_name | room_type | minimum_nights | host_id | price
-- 3176       | Cozy loft    | Entire    | 2              | 501     | 120.00
-- 4821       | Sunny studio | Private   | 1              | 501     | 85.00
-- 9012       | Beach house  | Entire    | 7              | 772     | 250.00
-- hosts (grain: one row per host)
-- host_id | host_name | is_superhost
-- 501     | Maria     | true
-- 772     | James     | false

The course project implements exactly this split. src_listings and src_hosts read separate raw sources; dim_listings_cleansed and dim_hosts_cleansed hold the cleansed dimension rows. Joining them back together for convenience is what dim_listings_w_hosts does in Section 8.

Why OLTP normalises and analytics denormalises

Operational databases (OLTP) optimise for fast, correct writes. Normalisation means updating a host name touches one row, not ten thousand listing rows. Referential integrity and row-level locking work cleanly when each entity has one home.

Analytics workloads (OLAP) optimise for fast reads across large scans. A star schema or a wide denormalised table lets a BI tool or a dashboard query answer a question with one or two joins instead of six. Columnar warehouses (Snowflake, BigQuery, Redshift, Databricks SQL) compress repeated dimension values efficiently, which is why the storage penalty of denormalisation is much smaller than it was on row-oriented OLTP systems.

Boyce-Codd normal form (BCNF)

BCNF is stricter than 3NF: every determinant must be a candidate key. In practice, almost nobody normalises analytics models beyond 3NF, and many OLTP schemas stop at 3NF too. The edge cases BCNF catches — odd functional dependencies involving overlapping composite keys — are rare in the business entities you model daily. Know the name for interviews; do not spend project time chasing BCNF unless you are designing a source system.

Functional dependency notation (for interviews)

Modelling textbooks write dependencies as arrows: host_id → host_name means host_name is determined by host_id. A 3NF violation exists when a non-key column depends on something that is not a candidate key for the table's grain. On a listings table at grain listing_id, the chain listing_id → host_id → host_name is transitive — host_name should move to the hosts table.

Denormalisation is a deliberate step, not a failure

Analytics engineers sometimes feel guilty building wide tables after learning normal forms. Denormalisation in the presentation layer is intentional: you already paid the cost of clean entities in staging and dimensions; joining them once into dim_listings_w_hosts or a mart saves every dashboard consumer from repeating that join. The mistake is denormalising before you have stable conformed dimensions — you embed inconsistent definitions that diverge silently.

dbt does not normalise for you. Staging models rename and cast columns; they do not automatically split entities. The decision to decompose listings and hosts into separate models is a modelling choice you make explicitly, then enforce with ref() and tests.

Facts and dimensions

Facts record what happened — measurements and events. Dimensions describe the context you filter and group by. The distinction is not about table size; it is about role in a query.

What makes a fact table

A fact table sits at the centre of a dimensional model. Its rows represent business events or measurements at a declared grain. Fact columns fall into two buckets:

  • Foreign keys — pointers to dimension tables (listing_id, host_id, date_key).
  • Measures — numeric columns you aggregate (review_sentiment encoded as a score, revenue, quantity). Some facts carry degenerate dimensions — attributes like an invoice number that belong at fact grain but are not worth a full dimension table.

What makes a dimension table

Dimensions are the who, what, where, when descriptors. They are relatively wide, relatively stable, and heavily reused. You filter on them (WHERE room_type = 'Entire home/apt'), group by them (GROUP BY host_name), and join them to facts. In the Airbnb project, dim_listings_cleansed and dim_hosts_cleansed are dimensions.

fct_reviews is a classic transaction fact: each row is one guest review, append-only in normal operation, with foreign keys to the listing dimension and a natural event timestamp in review_date. That profile makes it the course's incremental model candidate in Section 8.

Periodic and accumulating snapshot facts (when you outgrow transactions)

Transaction facts like fct_reviews are the right starting point. As the business matures you may need periodic snapshots — one row per listing per month with occupancy rate at month end — or accumulating snapshots — one row per booking tracking confirmation date, payment date, and check-in date as columns that update until the process completes. Neither appears in the course project, but interviewers often ask the difference. The rule of thumb: transaction for events, periodic for regular status readings, accumulating for long-running processes with milestones.

Compare to the fact: dimensions hold attributes you describe entities with; facts hold foreign keys pointing at those entities plus the event itself. fct_reviews carries listing_id but not room_type — that lives on dim_listings_cleansed and is joined when needed.

Factless fact tables in more detail

Factless facts record that a relationship or event occurred without a numeric measure. Examples: attendance (was the student present?), coverage (which product was promoted in which store?), or bridge tables linking dimensions in many-to-many relationships. You still declare grain and often COUNT rows as the implicit measure. The Airbnb project does not use a factless fact, but promotion-eligibility or listing-amenity coverage would be natural candidates if the dataset grew.

Dimensions answer “who/what/where”; facts answer “how many/how much”. If a column is something you would put in a WHERE or GROUP BY for business users, it probably belongs on a dimension. If it is something you SUM or COUNT, it belongs on a fact — or is derived from one at query time.

Star and snowflake schemas

A star schema puts one fact table at the centre with dimensions joined directly to it. A snowflake schema normalises those dimensions further into sub-dimensions. Both are valid; the choice is a trade-off between join count, storage, and maintainability.

Why columnar warehouses changed the calculus

Row-oriented OLTP databases paid heavily for wide tables: every query read every column. Columnar storage reads only the columns you select, and repeated dimension values compress well. That makes OBT and wide marts viable in Snowflake and BigQuery in ways they were not on Postgres a decade ago.

OBT is still the wrong default when: the same dimension is shared across many facts (you will rebuild the same wide join repeatedly); attributes change independently (host name changes should not force rebuilding every fact); or you need conformed dimensions across teams. The course teaches star schema first because it scales to those cases; dim_listings_w_hosts shows where a controlled denormalisation helps.

When snowflake still wins

Snowflake schemas remain appropriate when a sub-dimension is shared by many dimensions and maintained centrally — a date dimension, a geography hierarchy (country → region → city), or a product category tree referenced by facts and multiple role-playing dimensions. The course Airbnb dataset is small enough that a star is simpler; enterprise retail or finance models often snowflake geography and product hierarchies while keeping facts at the centre.

Role-playing dimensions

The same physical dimension table can play multiple roles in one fact — order date versus ship date versus delivery date, all joining to dim_date with different foreign keys. In dbt you either join the same ref('dim_date') multiple times with aliases in a mart, or expose role-specific views. The conformed date dimension ensures fiscal calendars and holidays mean the same thing everywhere.

Everyday analogy — a star schema is a restaurant menu organised by course (starters, mains, desserts) with a separate wine list you combine at order time. OBT is a set menu printed on one card: faster to read, painful to reprint when one dish changes.

Grain, keys and conformed dimensions

Grain is the single most important decision in fact modelling. Get it wrong and every metric built on the table is wrong — and tests may still pass if nobody thought to check row counts after joins.

Declaring grain first

Before you name a single measure, finish this sentence: one row in this table represents ___. For fct_reviews, the answer is one guest review of one listing on one date by one reviewer. Every design choice flows from that statement. If you later discover two reviews can share the same date, reviewer, and text on different listings, your grain statement still holds because listing_id is part of the natural key.

Changing grain after launch is expensive. Halving grain (adding detail) requires a backfill. Coarsening grain loses information permanently.

Natural keys versus surrogate keys

A natural key is a business identifier: host_id from the source system, an order number, a composite of columns that uniquely identifies an event. Natural keys are meaningful but brittle — source systems reuse IDs across environments, merge records, or change formats.

A surrogate key is a warehouse-generated identifier with no business meaning. It stabilises joins when natural keys change, supports SCD Type 2 (multiple rows for one natural key), and gives facts a single-column foreign key instead of a four-column composite.

fct_reviews uses dbt_utils.generate_surrogate_key over listing_id, review_date, reviewer_name, and review_text. The macro hashes the concatenation into a single key column. Full macro details are in Section 13.

Never use a natural key that the source can reuse. If an source system deletes and recreates host_id = 501 as a different person, a Type 1 dimension silently attaches the new person's name to the old host's history. Surrogate keys and snapshots exist to handle exactly this.

Conformed dimensions

A conformed dimension is the same dimension table reused across multiple fact tables with consistent keys and definitions. dim_listings_cleansed should mean the same thing whether you join it to reviews, bookings, or payouts.

Without conformance, two teams both publish a “customer” table with different deduplication rules, different active flags, and different surrogate keys. Executive dashboards that blend their metrics double-count or drop revenue. dbt helps by giving one project one definition, referenced via ref() — but conformed dimensions across dbt projects still need explicit agreement on keys and scd handling.

Degenerate dimensions

A degenerate dimension is a descriptive attribute stored on the fact table because it is at fact grain but not worth a separate dimension — for example an order number on an order-lines fact when you never analyse orders independently of lines.

Junk dimensions

A junk dimension bundles low-cardinality flags and indicators from a fact row into one surrogate-keyed dimension to avoid wide facts with dozens of boolean columns — for example combining payment method, promotion flag, and channel into a single junk_key.

Enforcing grain with tests

Grain is not a comment — it is a contract. A unique test on the grain key fails the build when duplicates appear, which is preferable to shipping a dashboard that double-counts revenue. Relationship tests confirm foreign keys point at existing dimension rows. The course adds these in Section 11; the practice section below has you add a grain test to fct_reviews.

dbt test — grain violation

dbt test --select fct_reviews
09:14:02  Running with dbt=1.11.12
09:14:05  Failure in test unique_fct_reviews_review_id
09:14:05    Got 3 results, configured to fail if != 0
09:14:05  Completed with 1 error

Three duplicate review_id values mean the surrogate key definition is missing a distinguishing column, or upstream staging duplicated rows. Fix the model logic, not the test threshold.

Common Table Expressions

A CTE (WITH ... AS (...)) names a subquery at the top of a SQL statement. dbt models conventionally chain CTEs and end with a final SELECT — the pattern you will see in every file under models/src/ and models/dim/.

Syntax and the dbt CTE chain pattern

WITH step_one AS (
    SELECT ...
    FROM {{ ref('upstream_model') }}
),
step_two AS (
    SELECT ...
    FROM step_one
)
SELECT
    ...
FROM step_two

Each CTE is one transformation step with a readable name. New requirements add a CTE rather than nesting subqueries three levels deep. In code review you can discuss cleansed_hosts by name instead of “the subquery on line 47”.

Readability versus subqueries

Subqueries in the FROM clause work identically in most warehouses, but they read inside-out. CTEs read top-to-bottom like a script. dbt's compiled SQL is often inspected by analysts who do not live in the repo daily — CTEs are the community convention for that audience.

Are CTEs materialised or inlined?

It depends on the warehouse optimiser, and the answer changed over time for some engines.

WarehouseTypical behaviourPractical note
SnowflakeGenerally inlines CTEs into one planCourse target; no automatic materialisation
BigQueryInlines; may cache repeated CTE scans in some casesUse a dbt model instead of expecting CTE persistence
Postgres < 12CTE results materialised (optimization fence)Could hide performance issues; upgraded behaviour since PG 12
Postgres 12+Inlined by default unless MATERIALIZED keyword usedCloser to Snowflake behaviour

If a CTE is expensive and reused, promote it to a dbt model with its own materialization rather than relying on the warehouse to cache it.

Recursive CTEs

Recursive CTEs reference themselves to walk hierarchies — org charts, bill-of-materials, nested categories. Syntax adds a UNION ALL between a base case and a recursive case. They appear rarely in analytics dbt projects compared to staging chains, but they are the standard SQL answer when you cannot flatten a hierarchy at load time.

CTE scope and the ephemeral materialization

Staging models in this project use the ephemeral materialization (set in dbt_project.yml for the src: folder). At compile time, dbt inlines them as CTEs inside parent models rather than creating warehouse objects. That gives you the readability of named staging steps without the storage and rebuild cost of physical tables — covered fully in Section 8.

Slowly Changing Dimensions

Dimensions change. Hosts rename themselves, listings change price, customers move city. Slowly Changing Dimension (SCD) techniques define how you record those changes without lying about history.

SCD types 0 through 6

TypeWhat it doesWhat you can answerWhat you cannot answerStorage cost
Type 0Retain original value; ignore updatesWhat was true at first loadAny subsequent changeLowest — one row per entity
Type 1Overwrite in placeCurrent state onlyWhat the value used to beLow — one row per entity
Type 2Insert new row; close old row with validity rangeFull history; point-in-time joinsRequires careful join predicatesHigh — one row per change
Type 3Add previous-value column on same rowCurrent and one prior valueHistory beyond one stepLow — extra columns only
Type 4Current row in dim; history in separate tableFast current queries; history when neededTwo tables to maintainMedium — split current/history
Type 6Hybrid of 1 + 2 + 3Current value plus historical rows plus previous columnComplexity — three mechanisms to keep alignedHighest

Type 2 worked example — host renames on raw_hosts

Suppose host 501 is loaded as Maria. On 2024-06-01 she renames to Maria Santos. A dbt snapshot on raw_hosts implements Type 2 automatically.

Before the rename (one current row in the snapshot table):

-- id | NAME   | is_superhost | dbt_scd_id | dbt_valid_from      | dbt_valid_to | dbt_updated_at
-- 501 | Maria  | true         | abc-111    | 2024-01-15 08:00:00 | NULL         | 2024-01-15 08:00:00

After the rename (two rows — old closed, new current):

-- id | NAME          | is_superhost | dbt_scd_id | dbt_valid_from      | dbt_valid_to        | dbt_updated_at
-- 501 | Maria         | true         | abc-111    | 2024-01-15 08:00:00 | 2024-06-01 09:00:00 | 2024-06-01 09:00:00
-- 501 | Maria Santos  | true         | abc-222    | 2024-06-01 09:00:00 | NULL                | 2024-06-01 09:00:00

Point-in-time analysis joins on host_id and filters where the fact's timestamp falls between dbt_valid_from and dbt_valid_to (or dbt_valid_to IS NULL for current). Joining on host_id alone duplicates every fact row across all history rows — the classic Type 2 bug covered in the traps section.

How dbt implements Type 2 — snapshots

dbt snapshots detect changes in a source table or model and append Type 2 history rows. The course configures them in YAML — the modern form introduced in dbt 1.9. Full walkthrough is in Section 10.

snapshots:
  - name: scd_raw_listings
    relation: source('airbnb', 'listings')
    config:
      unique_key: id
      strategy: timestamp
      updated_at: updated_at
      hard_deletes: invalidate

The hosts source gets an identical pattern in snapshots/raw_hosts_snapshot.yml with name: scd_raw_hosts. strategy: timestamp compares updated_at to detect changes. hard_deletes: invalidate closes rows that disappear from the source rather than pretending they still exist.

Older tutorials use {% snapshot %} blocks in .sql files. That form still works on many versions but YAML is the current standard. The course repo uses YAML exclusively.

Type 1 in dbt — plain rebuilt dimensions

dim_hosts_cleansed is a Type 1 dimension today: each run rebuilds the table and overwrites host names. You see the current name only. If you need history, you read from scd_raw_hosts instead — or build a dimension that sources from the snapshot.

Type 4 — current table plus history table

Type 4 keeps a slim current-state dimension for fast joins while storing full history in a separate table — often the snapshot table itself. Analysts doing operational reporting hit the current table; auditors hit history. In dbt this is a modelling choice: dim_hosts_cleansed for current, scd_raw_hosts for history, with marts picking the appropriate source.

Type 6 — hybrid 1 + 2 + 3

Type 6 combines overwrite of a current-value column, historical rows for reporting, and a previous-value column on the current row. It answers more question types without joins but triples the maintenance surface. Rare in dbt projects unless regulatory reporting demands both current and one-step-back columns on the same row alongside full history.

Timestamp versus check snapshot strategies

StrategyRequiresDetects changes whenCourse usage

The Airbnb raw tables carry updated_at, so timestamp strategy is appropriate. Sources that only append audit logs or lack change metadata need check strategy with an explicit column list — otherwise snapshots either miss changes or fire on irrelevant column drift.

Point-in-time join pattern for Type 2

When you need historical accuracy — what was the host name when this review was written — the join must constrain validity:

SELECT
    r.review_id,
    r.review_date,
    h.host_name
FROM {{ ref('fct_reviews') }} r
JOIN {{ ref('dim_listings_cleansed') }} l
  ON r.listing_id = l.listing_id
JOIN {{ ref('scd_raw_hosts') }} h
  ON l.host_id = h.id
 AND r.review_date >= h.dbt_valid_from
 AND (r.review_date < h.dbt_valid_to OR h.dbt_valid_to IS NULL)

In practice you wrap this in a mart model so analysts never write the predicate by hand. For current-state reporting only, join to dim_hosts_cleansed and accept Type 1 semantics.

      unique_key: id

Folder defaults in dbt_project.yml encode the pattern: staging is ephemeral (inlined into parents), dimensions default to tables, facts carry a fact tag for selection. You override per model — dim_listings_cleansed is a view — when the access pattern demands it. Materialization trade-offs are the subject of Section 8; building the models themselves starts in Section 7.

Medallion architecture alignment

The dimensional patterns map cleanly onto medallion naming: raw sources are bronze, src_ models are silver staging, dim_ and fct_ are silver/gold modelled entities, and mart_ is gold presentation. dbt does not mandate medallion labels, but the course folder structure is a Kimball star schema expressed as dbt conventions — which is what most analytics engineering teams actually run in production.

The course eventually builds a denormalised dimension joining listings to hosts — star schema convenience without touching the fact table:

    SELECT
        *
    FROM
        {{ ref('dim_listings_cleansed') }}

    l.room_type,

Traps that catch people

  • Grain violation after a join — joining fct_reviews to a dimension at the wrong grain (for example a host dimension row duplicated per listing) silently multiplies review counts. Revenue and averages look plausible but are wrong. Always validate row counts before and after joins; add a unique test on the fact grain key.
  • Natural key reuse — assuming host_id from the source identifies one person forever. If the source reuses IDs, Type 1 dimensions attach new attributes to old history. Use snapshots or surrogate keys keyed on source + load metadata when IDs are not trustworthy.
  • Type 2 join without validity predicate — JOIN dim ON fact.host_id = dim.host_id returns four rows per fact if the host changed four times. You must filter fact.review_date >= dim.dbt_valid_from AND (fact.review_date < dim.dbt_valid_to OR dim.dbt_valid_to IS NULL), or use a current-row view.
  • SELECT * past a schema change — a fact model doing SELECT * from staging picks up a new column silently. Downstream contracts break, or worse, a duplicate column name shadows a measure. Explicit column lists in facts and dimensions.
  • Confusing snapshot with incremental — both touch only changed rows on some runs, but snapshots append history rows for Type 2; incrementals append new fact events. Using incremental on a mutable dimension loses history; using snapshot on an append-only fact table wastes scans.
  • Snowflake schema by accident — splitting every attribute into its own dbt model without a business reason multiplies joins and confuses BI users. Normalise only when a sub-entity is shared across multiple dimensions.
  • OBT for every consumer — building a separate wide table per dashboard duplicates maintenance. Conformed dimensions exist so one dim_hosts_cleansed serves every fact.
  • Testing the wrong grain — a unique test on listing_id in a reviews fact passes while duplicate reviews exist. Test the full grain: review_id or the composite natural key.
  • Ignoring hard deletes — without hard_deletes: invalidate, deleted source rows linger as current in the snapshot. Metrics include listings that no longer exist.

Production patterns

Teams that model well in dbt agree on grain statements in pull request templates — literally a field that says one row represents before a fact merges. They enforce it with a unique and not_null test on the grain key in schema.yml, run on every CI build via dbt build.

For SCDs, production teams split the problem: Type 1 conformed dimensions in dim_ for current-state reporting (fast, simple joins); Type 2 snapshots on sources that feed point-in-time marts or regulatory reports. They rarely implement Type 6 — three mechanisms to keep aligned is operational debt without a clear audience.

Pattern: current-state mart

Facts join to dim_* Type 1 models. Snapshots exist for audit but are not in the default BI semantic layer. Analysts who need history opt into a separate mart.

Anti-pattern: snapshot everything

Snapshotting append-only facts and rebuilding wide OBT marts nightly from scratch. Storage and scan costs compound; nobody can explain which table is canonical.

Interview questions

Answer each one out loud before opening it.

When would you use a snapshot instead of an incremental model?Comparison

Use a snapshot when the source row mutates in place and you need to preserve history — host renames, price changes, status flips. Snapshots implement SCD Type 2 by closing old rows and inserting new ones when tracked columns change.

Use an incremental model when the source is append-only at the grain you model — new reviews, new orders, new events. You add rows for new keys or timestamps; you do not rewrite history. fct_reviews is the textbook incremental case; scd_raw_hosts is the snapshot case.

If you used incremental on a mutable dimension, the next run would overwrite changed attributes and history would be lost. If you used a snapshot on reviews, you would scan the entire reviews table every run to detect changes that never happen in place — wasted cost.

A join against your Type 2 dimension returned four times the rows. Why?Troubleshooting

The join matched every fact row to every history row for the same natural key. Type 2 dimensions contain multiple rows per host_id — one per version — with validity ranges in dbt_valid_from and dbt_valid_to.

Fixing it requires a point-in-time predicate: constrain the fact's event timestamp to fall within the dimension row's validity window, or filter to current rows only (dbt_valid_to IS NULL) if you accept Type 1 semantics for that query. Better still, expose a dim_hosts_current view for default reporting and a separate historical mart for auditors.

How do you choose the grain of a fact table?Design

Start from the business process, not the source file layout. Ask what single observable event or measurement the business cares to count. For reviews, the event is a guest submitted a review — one row per review, not one row per listing per day.

Then stress-test: can two rows exist with the same proposed key? If yes, the grain is too coarse. Can one business event split across two rows? If yes, the grain is too fine. Document the sentence one row represents... in the model description and enforce with a unique test on the grain key.

Changing grain later is painful — coarsening loses detail; refining requires a backfill. Getting this right before the first dbt run saves quarters of rework.

What is the difference between a star schema and a snowflake schema?Fundamentals

In a star schema, dimensions connect directly to the fact table — one hop per dimension. In a snowflake schema, dimensions are normalised into hierarchies of sub-dimension tables, so a single query may join fact → product → category → department.

Star schemas trade storage duplication for simpler queries and better BI usability. Snowflake schemas reduce redundancy but add join complexity. Modern columnar warehouses tilt the default toward star or OBT because join cost dropped relative to storage savings.

Explain SCD Type 1 versus Type 2.Modelling

Type 1 overwrites the dimension row when an attribute changes. You see the current value only; historical reporting uses today's name for yesterday's events. Implementation in dbt is a table or view rebuilt each run — dim_hosts_cleansed.

Type 2 preserves history by inserting a new row and closing the previous one with validity timestamps. Point-in-time joins reproduce what you would have known on a given date. dbt implements this with snapshots producing dbt_valid_from, dbt_valid_to, and dbt_scd_id.

What is a conformed dimension and why does it matter?Design

A conformed dimension is shared across fact tables with the same keys, attributes, and definitions. dim_listings_cleansed should mean the same thing whether joined to reviews, bookings, or payouts.

Without conformance, two teams publish different customer tables, executives blend metrics in a dashboard, and numbers disagree with no single source of truth. dbt's ref() helps within one project; cross-project conformance needs governance — shared packages or a mesh contract.

When is a measure semi-additive?Fundamentals

A semi-additive measure can be summed across some dimensions but not others — typically time. Account balance is the classic example: summing balances across customers is meaningful; summing balances across days is not (you double-count).

Handle semi-additive measures with periodic snapshot facts, or compute end-of-period values with window functions rather than naive SUM across dates.

Why generate a surrogate key in fct_reviews instead of using the natural columns?SQL

The natural key is four columns — awkward for joins, foreign keys in downstream tools, and error messages. Hashing them into review_id gives a single-column grain key that is stable as long as the component list is correct.

Surrogate keys also decouple the warehouse from source system key formats and prepare you for SCD scenarios where the same business key maps to multiple dimension rows. See dbt_utils.generate_surrogate_key in the real fct_reviews.sql.

What is the role of CTEs in dbt models?SQL

CTEs structure a model as a readable pipeline: import sources in the first CTE, apply cleansed logic in the next, select into the final output. They replace nested subqueries and match how analytics engineers discuss transformations step by step.

They are not separate materializations unless the warehouse optimiser chooses to materialize them (Postgres before version 12 always did). Expensive reusable steps should become their own dbt models with explicit materializations.

Why normalise in the source layer but denormalise in marts?Design

Staging normalises entities so each attribute is defined once — a host name change updates one place. Facts and dimensions stay narrow and conformed. Marts denormalise for consumption: joining listing, host, and review columns into one wide table a BI tool can scan without seven joins.

The ELT pattern loads raw data cheaply, dbt enforces clean entities in the middle layers, and presentation marts pay the join cost once at build time rather than on every dashboard query.

Recap

  • Normalisation removes redundancy; OLTP systems normalise to 3NF, analytics often denormalises for read performance.
  • Facts are events or measurements at declared grain; dimensions are descriptive context for filter and group by.
  • fct_reviews is a transaction fact; dim_listings_cleansed and dim_hosts_cleansed are Type 1 dimensions.
  • Star schemas join facts directly to dimensions; snowflake schemas normalise dimensions further; OBT pre-joins everything for scan speed.
  • Declare grain first — one row represents — and enforce it with a unique test on the grain key.
  • Surrogate keys stabilise joins; dbt_utils.generate_surrogate_key builds them from natural key columns.
  • Conformed dimensions share keys and definitions across facts; without them, metrics diverge between teams.
  • dbt models use CTE chains for readability; warehouses usually inline CTEs — promote expensive steps to models.
  • SCD Type 1 overwrites; Type 2 preserves history with validity ranges — dbt snapshots implement Type 2.
  • Joining Type 2 dimensions requires a validity predicate or you duplicate fact rows silently.
  • Folder convention: src_ staging, dim_ dimensions, fct_ facts, mart_ presentation, snapshots/ for SCD Type 2.
  • Snapshots are YAML in dbt 1.9+ with hard_deletes: invalidate, not the older {% snapshot %} SQL blocks.
